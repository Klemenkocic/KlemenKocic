I do that today at Luminous Group, an AI consultancy in Munich. I want to work at Asana for two reasons.

First, AI Studio sits at the exact layer that decides whether AI becomes part of how a team actually works on a real Monday — or stays a demo no-one opens. The hard part of AI adoption is not the model. It is the workflow design, the trust-building, and the translation between business intent and what the system actually does. That is the work I find most interesting, and it's also the work I am best at.

Second, I have used Asana as a power user. At MetaKing Studios I ran a 10-person studio on Asana for two years — sprints, releases, cross-timezone coordination. I know how the product feels from the inside, and I have strong opinions about how AI should integrate with it. That gives me a head start in customer conversations.

Human + AI collaboration is the category I think about every day. It is the subject of the seven essays I have published with Luminous Group. I would like to do that work with Asana customers next.

### Tell us about a customer-facing technical project you led (STAR format)

**Situation:** A founder of a 40-person professional-services firm came to Luminous Group with the brief "we should use AI somewhere." Vague goal, no specific workflow in mind, six different vendors had pitched them on six different products.

**Task:** Translate that vague intent into a specific AI workflow the firm would actually adopt — not a deck, an in-production system.

**Action:** I ran a 90-minute capability-assessment workshop with the leadership team, mapping where judgement actually happened in their operations versus where execution did. From the workshop we identified two workflows where AI augmentation made sense and two where it did not. I then designed and helped build the AI-augmented version of one workflow, including the human-approval gates, the failure-mode handling, and the way feedback would surface back to operators.

**Result:** The firm adopted the workflow firm-wide within six weeks. Two of their senior people now treat the system as a default part of their daily work. Equally important — the firm has a framework for evaluating future AI proposals against, so they stop being sold things they don't need.

### What's a non-obvious thing you've learned about AI in production?

The hard part of building an AI product is not getting the LLM to do something. It is deciding what the LLM should not do. At ViaVia, Charles passes through six layers of filtering before the model sees the data. Most of what makes the product feel reliable is the deterministic code around the model, not the prompts inside it. The same principle applies to enterprise AI: the customers who succeed are the ones who get specific about what AI is for, and equally specific about what it isn't.
